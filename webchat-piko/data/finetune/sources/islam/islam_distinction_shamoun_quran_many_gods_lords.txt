The Quran's Many Gods and Lords: An Analysis of the Worship given to And Deification of Creatures within the Islamic Text

Muslims boast that Islam is the most pure form of monotheism of all the monotheistic faiths. They similarly boast that the Quran is the most eloquent of all the religious texts and the only one perfectly preserved by Allah. They further claim that the Arabic of the Quran is clear and perfect. It is our intention here to examine these claims and see whether there is any merit to them.

The Quran on Creature Worship

There are several places in the Quran where creatures receive the worship due to God. In Q. 2:34, 15:28-32, angels are commanded to prostrate before/worship Adam. The word used (sujud/sajda) is the same term used in relation to the worship given to Allah. Masjid means a place where prostration and worship takes place. Allah commanded his creatures to render unto another creature the very worship, reverence and prostration due to God alone. In other words, Allah commanded his slaves to commit idolatry by having them show the same kind of worship to a creature as to the Creator. The Quran is hopelessly inconsistent: it teaches that prostration is only for God since it signifies worship, but then records acts of prostrations given to prophets with Allah commanding this.

In Q. 48:9, the antecedent of the pronouns (honor him, revere him, glorify him) can grammatically refer to Muhammad rather than Allah. Some Muslim commentators admit the text is ambiguous and that Muslims have disagreed whether the pronouns refer to Allah or the Prophet. The passage exposes the thoroughly human origin of the text—God would not have formulated it that way.

The Quran Bears Witness That There Are Other Gods Besides Allah

Q. 3:18: "God bears witness that there is no god but He—AND the angels, AND men possessed of knowledge." Because of the Arabic conjunction "wa" connecting the three groups, the literal reading has Allah testifying that he, the angels and the men of knowledge are all God. The author wrongly positioned the angels and men of knowledge in the sentence. Instead of having these groups confessing along with Allah, the author made them part of Allah's confession.

Q. 9:31: "They have taken their rabbis and their monks as lords apart from God AND the Messiah Mary's son." Because of the Arabic wording, the passage can be read as saying that Jesus is Lord along with Allah—the Quran's author ended up confessing that both Allah and Jesus are Lord. Muslim scholar Qadi 'Iyad noted that the conjunction "wa" is "the conjunction of partnership" and "it is not permitted to use this conjunction in connection with Allah in the case of anyone except the Prophet." Yet the Quran uses it to link Allah with angels, Jesus, and men of knowledge—implying equality/partnership.

The dilemma for the Muslim: If the Quran is 100% divine, one must take these verses literally, which would mean Allah has commanded idolatry or confessed to multiple gods. If one interpolates the "intended" meaning, one sacrifices belief in the purely divine nature of the Quran. The text exposes either theological incoherence or human authorship.
